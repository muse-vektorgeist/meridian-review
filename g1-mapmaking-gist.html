The Lost Art of Mapmaking

By Samuel Adeyemi · 9 min read · September 2026

Before satellites, a map was an argument. Cartographers decided what mattered — which rivers were drawn thick, which towns earned a dot, where the dragons went — and every choice was a small act of persuasion about what the world was.

The tools were humble: compass, chain, plane table, and an almost unreasonable patience. Surveying a coastline meant walking it, sighting it in triangles, and reducing months of field notes to a copper plate engraved by hand. A single chart could take a decade from first sighting to printed sheet.

"Every map is a compromise between the territory and the hand that draws it."

Errors propagated beautifully. A misplaced island on one chart was copied onto the next, and the next, until phantom islands — real places on paper, nowhere on earth — survived for centuries. Sailors reported them, expeditions searched for them, and cartographers, reluctant to delete what their predecessors had drawn, kept them alive.

GPS ended the romance and the errors together. Modern maps are databases rendered on demand, precise to the centimeter and innocent of argument. What we lost wasn't accuracy — we gained that — but the visible human hand: the knowledge that someone walked this coast, decided what it meant, and drew it so you could follow.
