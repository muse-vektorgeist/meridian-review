A Brief History of Lighthouse Keeping

For two hundred years, the most important job on the coast was also the loneliest. Lighthouse keepers tended flames, then oil lamps, then enormous Fresnel lenses — glass beehives that could throw a beam twenty miles to sea — in towers deliberately built where no one wanted to live.

The work was ritual as much as labor. Lamps trimmed at dusk, lenses polished until they rang, logbooks kept in handwriting so precise it looked printed. Keepers measured their lives in watches: four hours on, eight off, the light never unattended. Storms were the job at its purest — waves breaking over the gallery rail while the keeper climbed, lamp by lamp, to keep the flame alive.

"The light does not sleep, so neither did we. That was the whole of the law."

Automation arrived slowly, then all at once. The great lenses were replaced by sealed beams, then LEDs; foghorns gave way to radio beacons, then GPS. By the turn of the millennium, the last staffed lighthouses in most countries had gone dark — not extinguished, but unmanned, their keepers retired with pensions and stories.

What remains is mostly heritage now: museums in the keeper's cottages, tourists climbing the spiral stairs. But on a few remote rocks, the old towers still work — solar-powered, satellite-monitored, visited twice a year by a maintenance crew. The light still doesn't sleep. It just doesn't need anyone to watch it anymore.
