How Sourdough Starters Actually Work

By Theo Almeida · 6 min read · September 2026

That jar on your counter is not an ingredient. It is a managed ecosystem — a slow-motion negotiation between wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria, conducted in flour and water, and it has opinions about your schedule.

The cast is small but specific. Saccharomyces exiguus and its cousins do the lifting, producing carbon dioxide; Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis and fellow bacteria produce the acids that give sourdough its tang and, crucially, keep invading microbes out. The acidity is the security system. A healthy starter sits around pH 3.5 to 4 — sour enough to be inhospitable to almost everything except its own residents.

"You don't bake the bread. You conduct the orchestra, and the orchestra is microscopic."

Feeding the starter is ecosystem management: fresh flour dilutes the acids, which lets the yeast surge before the bacteria re-acidify the jar. Skip feedings and the bacteria win — the starter turns harsh, hooch (that dark liquid) pools on top, and the yeast goes dormant. The fix is almost always the same: discard, feed, wait. The community rebalances itself within days.

Every starter eventually becomes local. Studies of bakery starters show the microbial mix converging on strains from the flour, the water, even the baker's hands. Your bread tastes like your kitchen because, biologically speaking, it is your kitchen — cultured, fed, and baked at 450 degrees.
